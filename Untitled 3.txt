# Part A — Application Area Review

## Literature Review: Customer Churn Prediction in Telecommunications

### 1. Introduction to Customer Churn

Customer churn, also known as customer attrition, refers to the phenomenon where customers discontinue their relationship with a service provider [1]. In the telecommunications industry, churn represents a critical business challenge with significant financial implications. Acquiring new customers typically costs five to seven times more than retaining existing ones [5], making churn prediction and prevention a strategic priority for telecom operators worldwide.

The global telecommunications market has become increasingly competitive, with customers having multiple options for mobile, broadband, and bundled services. This competition, combined with reduced switching costs and number portability regulations, has elevated churn rates to concerning levels—often ranging from 20% to 40% annually in some markets [2]. Consequently, telecom companies have invested heavily in predictive analytics and machine learning solutions to identify at-risk customers before they leave.

### 2. Common Features and Data Sources

Churn prediction models in telecommunications typically leverage several categories of features derived from diverse data sources:

**Demographic Features:** Customer age, gender, geographic location, and household composition provide baseline segmentation. Senior citizens, for instance, may exhibit different usage patterns and service preferences [1].

**Account Information:** Contract type (month-to-month, annual, two-year), tenure with the company, payment method, and billing preferences are strong predictors. Month-to-month contracts consistently show higher churn rates due to lower switching barriers [2].

**Service Subscription Data:** The portfolio of services subscribed—including internet service type (DSL, fibre), phone services, streaming services, online security, and technical support—reveals customer engagement depth. Customers with multiple services typically demonstrate lower churn propensity [1].

**Usage Patterns:** Call detail records (CDRs), data consumption metrics, and service interaction frequencies provide behavioural insights. Declining usage often precedes churn decisions.

**Financial Metrics:** Monthly charges, total charges, and payment history indicate customer value and potential dissatisfaction with pricing.

**Customer Service Interactions:** Support ticket frequency, complaint history, and resolution times can signal underlying satisfaction issues.

Data sources typically include Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems, billing databases, network usage logs, and customer service records. The Kaggle Telco Customer Churn dataset [1] used in this project contains 7,043 customer records with 21 features spanning demographics, account information, and service subscriptions.

### 3. Machine Learning Techniques in Industry and Performance Results

The telecom industry employs various machine learning approaches for churn prediction, with published results demonstrating their effectiveness:

**Traditional Statistical Methods:** Logistic regression remains popular due to its interpretability and regulatory compliance advantages. It provides probability estimates and coefficient-based explanations that business stakeholders can understand [2]. **Performance:** Ahmad et al. (2019) report logistic regression achieving 76-79% accuracy on telecom churn datasets, with F1-scores of 0.68-0.72 for the minority (churn) class [2].

**Tree-Based Methods:** Decision Trees and ensemble methods like Random Forests and Gradient Boosting have gained widespread adoption due to their ability to capture non-linear relationships and feature interactions without extensive preprocessing. **Performance:** Single Decision Trees achieve 78-82% accuracy, while Random Forest ensembles reach 82-88% accuracy with ROC-AUC scores of 0.85-0.92 [2][8]. They provide interpretable feature importance rankings while handling non-linear patterns effectively.

**Neural Networks:** Deep learning approaches, including Multi-Layer Perceptrons and more complex architectures, can model intricate patterns but require larger datasets and sacrifice interpretability. **Performance:** MLPs achieve 80-86% accuracy on churn datasets with sufficient training data (>10,000 samples), though performance gains over tree-based methods are often marginal (1-3%) on structured tabular data [2]. Their application is growing as telecom companies accumulate more data.

**Gradient Boosting Methods:** XGBoost and LightGBM represent state-of-the-art for structured data. **Performance:** Studies report 85-90% accuracy and ROC-AUC scores of 0.88-0.94, though at the cost of interpretability [8].

**Survival Analysis:** Time-to-event models complement classification approaches by predicting when a customer might churn, enabling more targeted intervention timing.

### 4. Responsible Use, Privacy, and Governance

The deployment of churn prediction models raises important ethical and governance considerations:

**Privacy Concerns:** Churn models often incorporate sensitive personal data, including demographics, financial information, and behavioural patterns. Compliance with data protection regulations such as GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) requires explicit consent for data processing, purpose limitation, and data minimisation principles. Customers have rights to understand how their data informs automated decisions affecting their service [3].

**Algorithmic Fairness:** Models may inadvertently discriminate against protected groups. If churn predictions correlate with demographic factors like age or gender, subsequent retention interventions could create disparate treatment. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework [3] and EEOC guidelines [4] emphasise the need for fairness audits and bias mitigation.

**Transparency and Explainability:** Regulatory frameworks increasingly require that organisations explain automated decisions to affected individuals. Black-box models pose compliance challenges, favouring interpretable approaches or supplementary explanation techniques (SHAP, LIME) for complex models.

**Human Oversight:** Best practices recommend human-in-the-loop review for high-stakes decisions. Retention offers based purely on model predictions without human judgment may lead to inappropriate interventions or miss contextual factors.

**Model Governance:** Organisations should maintain documentation of model development, validation results, and ongoing monitoring. Regular audits for performance drift and bias emergence ensure continued responsible operation.

# Part B — Compare and Evaluate Three Techniques

## Technique 1: Logistic Regression

### Applicability
Logistic Regression is a classical statistical method for binary classification, making it directly applicable to churn prediction (Churn = Yes/No). It models the probability of churn as a logistic function of input features, establishing a linear decision boundary in the feature space. This technique excels when the relationship between predictors and the log-odds of churn is approximately linear [2].

### Required Input and Expected Output
**Input:** Logistic Regression requires numeric features. Categorical variables must be encoded (one-hot or ordinal encoding), and feature scaling is recommended for faster convergence, though not strictly necessary for correctness.

**Output:** Produces probability scores (0-1) for each class and binary predictions. Coefficients indicate feature importance with direction and magnitude.

### Data Availability and Practical Considerations
- **Data Availability:** Works effectively with standard CRM and billing data commonly available in telecom systems. Performs reasonably even with limited samples (hundreds to thousands).
- **Setup Time:** Minimal—no complex hyperparameter tuning required. Default parameters often suffice.
- **Time to Produce Results:** Training completes in seconds even on large datasets. Prediction is instantaneous.
- **Output Quality:** Produces well-calibrated probability estimates. Typical accuracy ranges 75-85% for churn datasets, though recall for minority class may suffer without class balancing.

### Pros and Cons
**Pros:**
- Highly interpretable coefficients enable business stakeholders to understand drivers
- Fast training and inference
- Low computational requirements
- Robust to small datasets and less prone to overfitting
- Probability outputs are naturally calibrated

**Cons:**
- Assumes linear relationship in log-odds space—may miss complex interactions
- Performance ceiling when non-linear patterns dominate
- Sensitive to multicollinearity among features
- Limited capacity for feature interaction without manual engineering

### Governance and Interpretability Tradeoffs
Logistic Regression offers excellent interpretability, making it ideal for regulated environments where decisions must be explained. Coefficients directly quantify how each feature influences churn probability, supporting GDPR Article 22 compliance for automated decision explanation. However, this interpretability comes at the cost of predictive power when underlying patterns are non-linear.
## Technique 2: Decision Tree (Chosen Prototype)

### Applicability
Decision Tree is a non-parametric supervised learning method used for classification that learns decision rules from data features. It creates a tree structure where internal nodes represent tests on features, branches represent decision outcomes, and leaf nodes represent class labels. Decision Trees are highly applicable to churn prediction due to their ability to capture non-linear relationships, feature interactions, and handle mixed data types after encoding [2]. This technique serves as our primary prototype due to its balance of interpretability and performance.

**Note:** Decision Trees form the foundation of ensemble methods like Random Forests (which combine multiple trees). Our single-tree implementation demonstrates the core algorithmic principles while remaining highly interpretable.

### Required Input and Expected Output
**Input:** Decision Trees can handle both numeric and encoded categorical features. They are robust to feature scaling (not required) and can manage missing values through surrogate splits in some implementations. One-hot encoding for categoricals ensures consistent treatment.

**Output:** Produces class predictions and probability estimates (from leaf node class proportions). Provides feature importance scores based on impurity reduction (Gini importance).

### Data Availability and Practical Considerations
- **Data Availability:** Highly flexible—performs well with typical telecom dataset sizes (thousands to millions of records). Can handle high-dimensional feature spaces effectively.
- **Setup Time:** Moderate—requires tuning of key hyperparameters (max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf) via cross-validation or grid search.
- **Time to Produce Results:** Training takes seconds on datasets of 5,000-10,000 samples. Much faster than neural networks.
- **Output Quality:** Typically achieves 75-82% accuracy on churn datasets. Feature importances offer actionable insights for business decisions.

### Pros and Cons
**Pros:**
- Captures non-linear relationships and feature interactions automatically
- Highly interpretable—can visualize the entire decision path
- Provides feature importance rankings for business insights
- Handles imbalanced classes via class_weight or sample_weight parameter
- Robust to outliers and does not require feature scaling
- Fast training and prediction

**Cons:**
- Prone to overfitting without proper pruning (max_depth, min_samples)
- Can create overly complex trees on noisy data
- Unstable—small changes in data can produce different trees
- Feature importance may be biased toward high-cardinality features
- Importance indicates predictive value, not causal relationships

### Governance and Interpretability Tradeoffs

Decision Trees offer excellent interpretability—individual predictions can be traced through explicit decision rules (if-then statements). This transparency makes them suitable for regulated environments where decisions must be explained to customers. Feature importances provide clear guidance on key churn drivers. However, overly complex trees become harder to interpret, requiring pruning for regulatory compliance. The model's explainability makes it ideal where transparency is valued, though ensemble methods (Random Forests) may offer better accuracy at the cost of interpretability.### Governance and Interpretability TradeoffsRandom Forest offers a middle ground between interpretability and performance. While individual predictions are harder to explain than logistic regression, aggregate feature importances provide directional guidance on key drivers. For regulatory compliance, supplementary techniques like SHAP values can generate local explanations. The model's robustness and accuracy make it a practical choice where some interpretability can be traded for predictive gains.
## Technique 3: Neural Network (MLPClassifier)

### Applicability
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward artificial neural network capable of learning complex, non-linear decision boundaries through multiple hidden layers of interconnected neurons. For churn prediction, MLPs can potentially capture intricate patterns in customer behaviour that simpler models miss [2]. However, their power comes with increased complexity and reduced interpretability.

### Required Input and Expected Output
**Input:** MLPs require carefully preprocessed numeric features. All inputs must be scaled (standardisation essential) as neural networks are sensitive to feature magnitudes. Categorical variables require one-hot encoding.

**Output:** Produces probability scores via softmax output layer and binary predictions. Internal representations (hidden layer activations) encode learned patterns but are not directly interpretable.

### Data Availability and Practical Considerations
- **Data Availability:** Requires larger datasets for reliable generalisation. With small telecom datasets (thousands of records), overfitting risk is significant without careful regularisation.
- **Setup Time:** High—requires tuning of architecture (hidden layer sizes, activation functions), learning rate, regularisation strength, and training epochs. Multiple training runs may be needed to find stable solutions.
- **Time to Produce Results:** Training is the slowest among the three techniques, potentially minutes even on modest datasets. Iterative optimisation through many epochs is computationally intensive.
- **Output Quality:** Potentially highest accuracy when sufficient data and proper tuning are available. However, performance gains over Decision Trees and ensemble methods are often marginal on structured tabular data, and models may produce poorly calibrated probabilities.

### Pros and Cons
**Pros:**
- Can learn highly complex, non-linear patterns
- Flexible architecture adaptable to problem complexity
- Potential for highest accuracy with sufficient data
- Can benefit from transfer learning and pre-training in advanced applications

**Cons:**
- Black-box nature makes interpretation extremely difficult
- Requires careful hyperparameter tuning and architecture design
- Sensitive to feature scaling and initialisation
- Prone to overfitting on small datasets
- Longer training times and higher computational cost
- May require GPU for larger datasets

### Governance and Interpretability Tradeoffs
MLPs present the greatest governance challenge due to their black-box nature. Individual predictions cannot be easily explained without supplementary techniques (SHAP, LIME, integrated gradients). In regulated environments, this opacity may conflict with transparency requirements. The interpretability cost should only be accepted when MLPs provide substantial accuracy improvements over more explainable alternatives—which is often not the case for structured tabular data like telecom churn datasets.

class Scaler:
    def __init__(self):
        self.mean = None
        self.std = None
        self.mean_ = None   
        self.scale_ = None   
        self.n_samples_seen_ = None  # Track number of samples seen during fit
    
    def fit(self, X):
        # Learn mean and std from training data
        X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        self.mean = X.mean(axis=0)
        self.std = X.std(axis=0)
        # Avoid dividing by zero for constant features
        self.std[self.std == 0] = 1.0
        # Store sklearn-compatible attributes for testing
        self.mean_ = self.mean
        self.scale_ = self.std
        self.n_samples_seen_ = X.shape[0]  # Number of samples used to fit
        return self
    
    def transform(self, X):
        # Apply learned scaling
        X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        return (X - self.mean) / self.std
    
    def fit_transform(self, X):
        self.fit(X)
        return self.transform(X)


class CategoricalEncoder:
    def __init__(self):
        self.categories = {}
        self.feature_names = []
    
    def fit(self, X):
        if not isinstance(X, pd.DataFrame):
            raise TypeError("Need a DataFrame here")
        
        self.categories.clear()
        self.feature_names.clear()
        
        # Store unique values for each column
        for col in X.columns:
            values = sorted(X[col].astype(str).unique())
            self.categories[col] = values
            # Build feature names like "Contract_Monthly"
            for v in values:
                self.feature_names.append(col + "_" + v)
        
        return self
    
    def transform(self, X):
        X = X.copy()
        encoded_cols = []
        
        # Create binary columns for each category
        for col, values in self.categories.items():
            col_data = X[col].astype(str).values
            out = np.zeros((len(col_data), len(values)))
            
            for i, v in enumerate(values):
                out[:, i] = (col_data == v).astype(float)
            
            encoded_cols.append(out)
        
        return np.hstack(encoded_cols)
    
    def fit_transform(self, X):
        self.fit(X)
        return self.transform(X)


def split_data(X, y, test_size=0.2, seed=None, stratify=None):
     
    if seed is not None:
        np.random.seed(seed)
    
    n = len(X)
    
    if stratify is None:
        # Simple random split
        indices = np.arange(n)
        np.random.shuffle(indices)
        split_point = int(n * test_size)
        test_idx = indices[:split_point]
        train_idx = indices[split_point:]
    else:
        # Stratified split - maintain class proportions
        train_idx = []
        test_idx = []
        
        # Get unique classes and their indices
        unique_classes = np.unique(stratify)
        
        for class_label in unique_classes:
            # Find all indices for this class
            class_indices = np.where(stratify == class_label)[0]
            
            # Shuffle indices for this class
            np.random.shuffle(class_indices)
            
            # Calculate split point for this class
            n_class = len(class_indices)
            n_test = int(n_class * test_size)
            
            # Split this class
            test_idx.extend(class_indices[:n_test])
            train_idx.extend(class_indices[n_test:])
        
        # Convert to arrays and shuffle to mix classes
        train_idx = np.array(train_idx)
        test_idx = np.array(test_idx)
        np.random.shuffle(train_idx)
        np.random.shuffle(test_idx)
    
    # Extract the split data using iloc for DataFrame compatibility
    X_train = X.iloc[train_idx].reset_index(drop=True)
    X_test = X.iloc[test_idx].reset_index(drop=True)
    y_train = y.iloc[train_idx].reset_index(drop=True)

    y_test = y.iloc[test_idx].reset_index(drop=True)

    print("Preprocessing classes defined")

    return X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test

# Evaluation Metrics - implementing these myself since we can't use sklearn

def confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred):
    # Build confusion matrix: [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]]
    y_true, y_pred = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_pred)
    tn = np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 0))
    fp = np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 1))
    fn = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 0))
    tp = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))
    return np.array([[tn, fp], [fn, tp]])

def accuracy(y_true, y_pred):
    # Simple: correct predictions / total predictions
    return np.mean(np.array(y_true) == np.array(y_pred))

def precision(y_true, y_pred):
    # Of predicted positives, how many are actually positive
    y_true, y_pred = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_pred)
    tp = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))
    fp = np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 1))
    return tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) > 0 else 0.0

def recall(y_true, y_pred):
    # Of actual positives, how many did we catch
    y_true, y_pred = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_pred)
    tp = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))
    fn = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 0))
    return tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) > 0 else 0.0

def f1_score(y_true, y_pred):
    # Harmonic mean of precision and recall
    p, r = precision(y_true, y_pred), recall(y_true, y_pred)
    return 2 * p * r / (p + r) if (p + r) > 0 else 0.0

def roc_auc(y_true, y_prob):
    # Calculate ROC AUC manually using trapezoidal rule
    # Sort by predicted probability (highest first)
    y_true, y_prob = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_prob)
    sorted_idx = np.argsort(y_prob)[::-1]
    y_sorted = y_true[sorted_idx]
    
    n_pos, n_neg = np.sum(y_true == 1), np.sum(y_true == 0)
    if n_pos == 0 or n_neg == 0:
        return 0.5  # Edge case - can't compute AUC
    
    # Build ROC curve points
    tpr, fpr = [0.0], [0.0]
    tp, fp = 0, 0
    
    for label in y_sorted:
        if label == 1:
            tp += 1
        else:
            fp += 1
        tpr.append(tp / n_pos)
        fpr.append(fp / n_neg)
    
    # Integrate using trapezoid rule (area under curve)
    auc = sum((fpr[i] - fpr[i-1]) * (tpr[i] + tpr[i-1]) / 2 for i in range(1, len(fpr)))
    return auc

def roc_curve(y_true, y_prob):
    # Calculate ROC curve points for plotting
    y_true, y_prob = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_prob)
    thresholds = np.sort(np.unique(y_prob))[::-1]
    n_pos, n_neg = np.sum(y_true == 1), np.sum(y_true == 0)
    
    fpr_list, tpr_list = [0.0], [0.0]
    for thresh in thresholds:
        y_pred = (y_prob >= thresh).astype(int)
        tp = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))
        fp = np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 1))
        tpr_list.append(tp / n_pos if n_pos > 0 else 0)
        fpr_list.append(fp / n_neg if n_neg > 0 else 0)
    
    fpr_list.append(1.0)
    tpr_list.append(1.0)
    return np.array(fpr_list), np.array(tpr_list), thresholds

def pr_curve(y_true, y_prob):
    # Calculate precision-recall curve
    y_true, y_prob = np.array(y_true), np.array(y_prob)
    thresholds = np.sort(np.unique(y_prob))[::-1]
    
    prec_list, rec_list = [1.0], [0.0]
    for thresh in thresholds:
        y_pred = (y_prob >= thresh).astype(int)
        tp = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))
        fp = np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 1))
        fn = np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 0))
        prec_list.append(tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) > 0 else 1.0)
        rec_list.append(tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) > 0 else 0.0)
    return np.array(prec_list), np.array(rec_list), thresholds

def pr_auc(y_true, y_prob):
    # Calculate average precision (area under PR curve)
    prec, rec, _ = pr_curve(y_true, y_prob)
    return abs(sum((rec[i] - rec[i-1]) * prec[i] for i in range(1, len(rec))))

def compute_all_metrics(y_true, y_pred, y_prob=None):
    # Helper to get all metrics at once
    metrics = {
        'Accuracy': accuracy(y_true, y_pred),
        'Precision': precision(y_true, y_pred),
        'Recall': recall(y_true, y_pred),
        'F1': f1_score(y_true, y_pred)
    }
    if y_prob is not None:
        metrics['ROC-AUC'] = roc_auc(y_true, y_prob)
        metrics['PR-AUC'] = pr_auc(y_true, y_prob)
    return metrics

class LogisticRegression:
    
    def __init__(self, learning_rate=0.01, max_iter=1000, class_weight='balanced', random_state=None):
        self.learning_rate = learning_rate
        self.max_iter = max_iter
        self.class_weight = class_weight
        self.random_state = random_state
        self.weights = None
        self.bias = 0.0
    
    def _sigmoid(self, z):
        
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -500, 500)))
    
    def _compute_class_weights(self, y):
       
        if self.class_weight != 'balanced':
            return np.ones(len(y))
        
        unique_classes, counts = np.unique(y, return_counts=True)
        n_samples = len(y)
        n_classes = len(unique_classes)
        
        # Weight formula: n_samples / (n_classes * count_for_class)
        class_weights = n_samples / (n_classes * counts)
        
        # Map class weights to each sample
        sample_weights = np.array([class_weights[int(label)] for label in y])
        return sample_weights
    
    def fit(self, X, y):
        if self.random_state is not None:
            np.random.seed(self.random_state)
        
        n_samples, n_features = X.shape
        
        # Initialize parameters
        self.weights = np.zeros(n_features)
        self.bias = 0.0
        
        # Compute class weights
        sample_weights = self._compute_class_weights(y)
        
        # Gradient descent optimization
        for iteration in range(self.max_iter):
            # Forward pass: compute predictions
            linear_output = X @ self.weights + self.bias
            predictions = self._sigmoid(linear_output)
            
            # Compute weighted error
            errors = (predictions - y) * sample_weights
            
            # Compute gradients
            weight_gradient = (X.T @ errors) / n_samples
            bias_gradient = np.sum(errors) / n_samples
            
            # Update parameters
            self.weights -= self.learning_rate * weight_gradient
            self.bias -= self.learning_rate * bias_gradient
        
        return self
    
    def predict_proba(self, X):
        linear_output = X @ self.weights + self.bias
        prob_positive = self._sigmoid(linear_output)
        prob_negative = 1 - prob_positive
        return np.column_stack([prob_negative, prob_positive])
    
    def predict(self, X, threshold=0.5):
        probabilities = self.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
        return (probabilities >= threshold).astype(int)

import time
import numpy as np

class DecisionTree:
    # Implements a basic classification tree using Gini impurity
    # Note: I'm not using recursion depth limits strictly for pruning, 
    # but to stop the tree from growing too deep and overfitting.
    
    def __init__(self, max_depth=10, min_samples_split=10, min_samples_leaf=5, seed=None):
        self.max_depth = max_depth
        self.min_samples_split = min_samples_split
        self.min_samples_leaf = min_samples_leaf
        self.seed = seed
        self.tree = None
        self.feature_importances_ = None
    
    def _calculate_gini(self, labels):
        # Formula: 1 - sum(p_i^2)
        # If the node is empty, impurity is 0
        total_samples = len(labels)
        if total_samples == 0:
            return 0
            
        # Get counts for 0s and 1s
        _, counts = np.unique(labels, return_counts=True)
        probs = counts / total_samples
        
        # Calculate Gini
        gini = 1 - np.sum(probs ** 2)
        return gini
    
    def _find_best_split(self, X_data, y_data):
        # Brute force search: check every feature and every threshold
        best_gain = -1
        best_feature_idx = None
        best_threshold_val = None
        
        current_uncertainty = self._calculate_gini(y_data)
        n_samples = len(y_data)
        
        n_features = X_data.shape[1]
        
        for feat_idx in range(n_features):
            # Get all unique values for this feature to try as thresholds
            possible_thresholds = np.unique(X_data[:, feat_idx])
            
            # optimization: if too many unique values (like continuous data), 
            # just check percentiles to save time
            if len(possible_thresholds) > 50:
                possible_thresholds = np.percentile(X_data[:, feat_idx], np.linspace(0, 100, 50))
            
            for thresh in possible_thresholds:
                # Mask for splitting
                left_mask = X_data[:, feat_idx] <= thresh
                
                # Split the targets
                y_left = y_data[left_mask]
                y_right = y_data[~left_mask]
                
                # Validation: Don't split if it results in tiny leaves
                if len(y_left) < self.min_samples_leaf or len(y_right) < self.min_samples_leaf:
                    continue
                
                # Calculate weighted Gini for children
                gini_left = self._calculate_gini(y_left)
                gini_right = self._calculate_gini(y_right)
                
                weighted_gini = (len(y_left) * gini_left + len(y_right) * gini_right) / n_samples
                
                # Information Gain = Old Gini - New Gini
                info_gain = current_uncertainty - weighted_gini
                
                # Keep track of the best split found so far
                if info_gain > best_gain:
                    best_gain = info_gain
                    best_feature_idx = feat_idx
                    best_threshold_val = thresh
        
        return best_feature_idx, best_threshold_val, best_gain
    
    def _grow_tree(self, X_subset, y_subset, current_depth=0):
        n_samples = len(y_subset)
        n_labels = len(np.unique(y_subset))
        
        # Stopping criteria (Base cases)
        # 1. Max depth reached
        # 2. Node is pure (only 1 class left)
        # 3. Not enough samples to split further
        if (current_depth >= self.max_depth or 
            n_labels == 1 or 
            n_samples < self.min_samples_split):
            
            # Create a leaf node with the majority class
            class_counts = np.bincount(y_subset.astype(int), minlength=2)
            majority_class = np.argmax(class_counts)
            probabilities = (class_counts / n_samples).tolist()
            return {'leaf': True, 'prediction': majority_class, 'probs': probabilities}
        
        # Find the split that maximizes Information Gain
        feat_idx, thresh, gain = self._find_best_split(X_subset, y_subset)
        
        # If no split improves the Gini (gain <= 0), make it a leaf
        if feat_idx is None or gain <= 0:
            class_counts = np.bincount(y_subset.astype(int), minlength=2)
            return {
                'leaf': True, 
                'prediction': np.argmax(class_counts), 
                'probs': (class_counts / n_samples).tolist()
            }
        
        # Record feature importance (Gini importance)
        self.feature_importances_[feat_idx] += gain * n_samples
        
        # Recursive step: build left and right subtrees
        left_indices = X_subset[:, feat_idx] <= thresh
        
        left_subtree = self._grow_tree(X_subset[left_indices], y_subset[left_indices], current_depth + 1)
        right_subtree = self._grow_tree(X_subset[~left_indices], y_subset[~left_indices], current_depth + 1)
        
        return {
            'leaf': False,
            'feature_index': feat_idx,
            'threshold': thresh,
            'left': left_subtree,
            'right': right_subtree
        }
    
    def fit(self, X, y):
        if self.seed:
            np.random.seed(self.seed)
            
        # Initialize importances to zero
        self.feature_importances_ = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
        
        # Start building the tree from the root
        self.tree = self._grow_tree(X, y)
        
        # Normalize importances so they sum to 1
        total_imp = self.feature_importances_.sum()
        if total_imp > 0:
            self.feature_importances_ /= total_imp
            
        return self
    
    def _traverse_tree(self, x, node):
        # Recursive function to walk down the tree for a single sample
        if node['leaf']:
            return node['prediction'], node['probs']
        
        if x[node['feature_index']] <= node['threshold']:
            return self._traverse_tree(x, node['left'])
        else:
            return self._traverse_tree(x, node['right'])
    
    def predict(self, X):
        predictions = [self._traverse_tree(x, self.tree)[0] for x in X]
        return np.array(predictions)
    
    def predict_proba(self, X):
        probs = [self._traverse_tree(x, self.tree)[1] for x in X]
        return np.array(probs)

class NeuralNetwork:
    # A simple MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) with one hidden layer.
    # Architecture: Input -> [Weights1] -> ReLU -> [Weights2] -> Sigmoid -> Output
    
    def __init__(self, hidden_size=64, lr=0.05, n_iter=2000, class_weight='balanced', seed=None):
        self.hidden_size = hidden_size
        self.lr = lr
        self.n_iter = n_iter
        self.class_weight = class_weight
        self.seed = seed
        # Weights and biases
        self.W1 = None 
        self.b1 = None
        self.W2 = None
        self.b2 = None
    
    def _sigmoid(self, z):
        # Clip to avoid overflow/underflow warnings in exp
        # Learned this fix from StackOverflow/Documentation when I got NaNs
        z_clipped = np.clip(z, -500, 500)
        return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z_clipped))
    
    def _relu(self, z):
        return np.maximum(0, z)
    
    def _relu_derivative(self, z):
        # Derivative is 1 where z > 0, else 0
        return (z > 0).astype(float)
    
    def _compute_weights(self, y_train):
        # Handle class imbalance by weighting the error term
        if self.class_weight != 'balanced':
            return np.ones(len(y_train))
            
        unique_classes, counts = np.unique(y_train, return_counts=True)
        # Formula: n_samples / (n_classes * count_of_class)
        weights_map = len(y_train) / (len(unique_classes) * counts)
        
        # Map back to sample array
        sample_weights = np.array([weights_map[int(label)] for label in y_train])
        return sample_weights.reshape(-1, 1)
    
    def fit(self, X, y):
        if self.seed:
            np.random.seed(self.seed)
            
        n_samples, n_features = X.shape
        
        # Xavier Initialization (Good for Tanh/Sigmoid, acceptable for ReLU)
        # Scales weights by 1/sqrt(n) to keep variance stable
        std_dev = np.sqrt(2.0 / n_features)
        self.W1 = np.random.randn(n_features, self.hidden_size) * std_dev
        self.b1 = np.zeros((1, self.hidden_size))
        
        std_dev_2 = np.sqrt(2.0 / self.hidden_size)
        self.W2 = np.random.randn(self.hidden_size, 1) * std_dev_2
        self.b2 = np.zeros((1, 1))
        
        # Prepare targets and weights
        y_col = y.reshape(-1, 1)
        sample_weights = self._compute_weights(y)
        
        # Training Loop
        for i in range(self.n_iter):
            # --- Forward Propagation ---
            # Layer 1 (Input -> Hidden)
            z1 = np.dot(X, self.W1) + self.b1
            a1 = self._relu(z1)
            
            # Layer 2 (Hidden -> Output)
            z2 = np.dot(a1, self.W2) + self.b2
            a2 = self._sigmoid(z2) # Predictions
            
            # --- Backpropagation ---
            # Calculate Error (Prediction - Actual) weighted by class importance
            error = (a2 - y_col) * sample_weights
            
            # Gradients for Layer 2
            # dW2 = (a1_transpose dot error) / n
            dW2 = np.dot(a1.T, error) / n_samples
            db2 = np.sum(error, axis=0, keepdims=True) / n_samples
            
            # Gradients for Layer 1
            # Propagate error back through the weights and activation derivative
            error_hidden = np.dot(error, self.W2.T)
            dz1 = error_hidden * self._relu_derivative(z1)
            
            dW1 = np.dot(X.T, dz1) / n_samples
            db1 = np.sum(dz1, axis=0, keepdims=True) / n_samples
            
            # --- Update Weights ---
            self.W1 = self.W1 - self.lr * dW1
            self.b1 = self.b1 - self.lr * db1
            self.W2 = self.W2 - self.lr * dW2
            self.b2 = self.b2 - self.lr * db2
            
        return self
    
    def predict_proba(self, X):
        # Forward pass only
        z1 = np.dot(X, self.W1) + self.b1
        a1 = self._relu(z1)
        z2 = np.dot(a1, self.W2) + self.b2
        a2 = self._sigmoid(z2)
        
        # Return [Prob_Class_0, Prob_Class_1]
        probs_1 = a2.flatten()
        return np.column_stack([1 - probs_1, probs_1])
    
    def predict(self, X, threshold=0.5):
        probs = self.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
        return (probs >= threshold).astype(int)

def bootstrap_metric(y_true, y_pred, y_prob, metric_func, n_iterations=1000, confidence=0.95):
    """Calculate bootstrap confidence interval for a metric."""
    np.random.seed(RANDOM_STATE)
    n_samples = len(y_true)
    metric_values = []
    
    for _ in range(n_iterations):
        # Resample with replacement
        indices = np.random.choice(n_samples, size=n_samples, replace=True)
        y_true_boot = y_true[indices]
        y_pred_boot = y_pred[indices]
        
        # Calculate metric
        if metric_func.__name__ in ['roc_auc', 'pr_auc']:
            y_prob_boot = y_prob[indices]
            metric_val = metric_func(y_true_boot, y_prob_boot)
        else:
            metric_val = metric_func(y_true_boot, y_pred_boot)
        
        metric_values.append(metric_val)
    
    # Calculate confidence interval
    alpha = 1 - confidence
    lower = np.percentile(metric_values, 100 * alpha / 2)
    upper = np.percentile(metric_values, 100 * (1 - alpha / 2))
    mean = np.mean(metric_values)
    
    return mean, lower, upper

def k_fold_split(X, y, n_folds=5, random_state=None):
    """Split data into k folds with stratification."""
    if random_state is not None:
        np.random.seed(random_state)
    
    n_samples = len(X)
    indices = np.arange(n_samples)
    
    # Stratified splitting
    fold_assignments = np.zeros(n_samples, dtype=int)
    
    # Get indices for each class
    for class_label in np.unique(y):
        class_indices = np.where(y == class_label)[0]
        np.random.shuffle(class_indices)
        
        # Assign to folds in round-robin fashion
        for i, idx in enumerate(class_indices):
            fold_assignments[idx] = i % n_folds
    
    # Create folds
    folds = []
    for fold_idx in range(n_folds):
        test_mask = fold_assignments == fold_idx
        train_mask = ~test_mask
        folds.append((train_mask, test_mask))
    
    return folds

def calibration_curve(y_true, y_prob, n_bins=10):
    """Calculate calibration curve."""
    bins = np.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1)
    true_probs, pred_probs = [], []
    
    for i in range(n_bins):
        if i == n_bins - 1:
            mask = (y_prob >= bins[i]) & (y_prob <= bins[i + 1])
        else:
            mask = (y_prob >= bins[i]) & (y_prob < bins[i + 1])
        if np.sum(mask) > 0:
            true_probs.append(np.mean(y_true[mask]))
            pred_probs.append(np.mean(y_prob[mask]))
    
    return np.array(true_probs), np.array(pred_probs)

# Fairness Analysis Functions
# Need to check if model treats different groups fairly

def compute_fairness_metrics(y_true, y_pred, group_labels, group_name):
    # Calculate metrics separately for each demographic group
    # This helps identify if the model is biased
    results = []
    groups = np.unique(group_labels)
    
    for group in groups:
        mask = group_labels == group
        n = np.sum(mask)
        
        y_true_g = y_true[mask]
        y_pred_g = y_pred[mask]
        
        # Selection rate: proportion predicted as positive (churn)
        selection_rate = np.mean(y_pred_g)
        
        # Calculate TPR, FPR, Precision
        tp = np.sum((y_true_g == 1) & (y_pred_g == 1))
        fn = np.sum((y_true_g == 1) & (y_pred_g == 0))
        fp = np.sum((y_true_g == 0) & (y_pred_g == 1))
        tn = np.sum((y_true_g == 0) & (y_pred_g == 0))
        
        tpr = tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) > 0 else 0  # True Positive Rate (Recall)
        fpr = fp / (fp + tn) if (fp + tn) > 0 else 0  # False Positive Rate
        precision = tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) > 0 else 0
        
        results.append({
            'Group': group,
            'N': n,
            'Selection_Rate': selection_rate,
            'TPR': tpr,
            'FPR': fpr,
            'Precision': precision,
            'Actual_Churn_Rate': np.mean(y_true_g)
        })
    
    return pd.DataFrame(results)


def compute_disparate_impact(fairness_df, reference_group):
    """
    Compute disparate impact ratio relative to reference group.
    Ratio = selection_rate(group) / selection_rate(reference)
    
    Acceptable range typically: 0.8 - 1.25 (four-fifths rule)
    """
    # Get reference group's selection rate
    ref_rate = fairness_df[fairness_df['Group'] == reference_group]['Selection_Rate'].values[0]
    
    # Calculate disparate impact: how much do selection rates differ?
    # Four-fifths rule: ratio should be between 0.8 and 1.25
    if ref_rate == 0:
        fairness_df['DI_Ratio'] = np.inf
    else:
        fairness_df['DI_Ratio'] = fairness_df['Selection_Rate'] / ref_rate
    
    # Flag groups outside acceptable range
    fairness_df['DI_Flag'] = fairness_df['DI_Ratio'].apply(
        lambda x: 'OK' if 0.8 <= x <= 1.25 else 'FLAG'
    )
    
    return fairness_df
